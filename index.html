Lecture 2: CNS and PNS Meningitis Overview
Category Details
Definition Meningitis — an inflammatory condition affecting the leptomeninges.
Types Acute (viral or bacterial) and Chronic (often fungal).
Common pathogens
  • Bacterial: Streptococcus pneumoniae (most common), Neisseria meningitidis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Staphylococcus aureus.
  • Viral: Enteroviruses (coxsackie, echovirus), Herpes simplex virus.
  • Fungal: Cryptococcus spp. (immunocompromised patients).
Pathophysiology / Routes of entry 1. Direct spread — through skin, nose, via anatomical defects or skull fractures.
2. Hematogenous spread — nasopharyngeal colonization → bloodstream → choroid plexus/CSF.
Risk factors
  • V: Vaccine refusal (bacterial)
  • I: immunosuppression
  • M: Medical conditions or devices (malignancy, VP shunt, HIV, splenectomy, IV drug use, renal failure).
  • E: Exposure /travel to endemic areas
  • A: Age (infants & elderly)
    		•
    Clinical features / Symptoms
  • Classic triad:
    • Fever,
    • Headache,
    • Neck stiffness (nuchal rigidity)
    May also have photophobia or phonophobia.
    Diagnosis Clinical assessment + lumbar puncture to evaluate CSF clarity, pressure, culture, WBCs, protein.
    Complications
  • Death
  • Seizures
  • Hydrocephalus (build up of pressure of CSF)
  • Hearing loss
    		•
    Prevention Vaccination for bacterial meningitis:
    • Meningococcal for meningitidis
    • Hib for H. influenzae B
    • Pneumococcal for Streptococcus pneumoniae
    Treatment
    • Bacterial: give Dexamethasone prior to antibiotics (to reduce leptomeningeal injury) then appropriate antibiotics.
    • Viral/fungal/other: specific antimicrobial/antiviral/antifungal therapy and supportive care as indicated.
    Acute vs Chronic note
  • Acute forms are usually bacterial/viral
  • Chronic forms are commonly fungal (especially in immunocompromised).

    Stroke type Definition / key features Common causes / risk factors Typical symptoms Management / notes
    Ischemic stroke Reduced or interrupted blood flow to brain → neuronal ATP depletion and necrosis → accounts for ~85% of strokes. Thrombus formation
  • emboli from elsewhere
  • carotid disease
  • atrial fibrillation
  • hypertension
  • diabetes
  • hyperlipidemia
  • smoking
  • hypercoagulable states.
    		•
  • Weakness
  • numbness
  • facial droop
  • slurred speech
  • language disturbance
  • vision changes
  • incoordination (depends on area).
    		•  Acute: thrombolysis / endovascular therapy when indicated. Chronic: secondary prevention (anticoagulants if cardioembolic
  • antiplatelets
  • antihypertensives
  • statins
  • rehab).
    		•
    Thrombotic stroke (a subtype of ischemic) Occlusion due to thrombus formed within cerebral vessels → may be preceded by TIAs.
  • Atherosclerotic plaque in cerebral/large vessels
  • small-vessel lipohyalinosis. Risk factors as for ischemic stroke.
  • D: dizziness
  • C: confusion
  • H: headache
  • I: Incoordination
  • L: Language/slurred speech
  • L: limb paralysis
  • S: Small transient events
    		•  May be preceded by TIAs
  • acute reperfusion strategies when eligible
  • address local vessel disease and risk factor control.
    		•
    Embolic stroke (a subtype of ischemic) Arterial occlusion from embolus originating elsewhere (heart
  • proximal arteries) causing sudden ischemia.
    •      		Atrial fibrillation
  • recent myocardial infarction
  • valvular disease
  • carotid plaque
  • paradoxical embolus.
    		•
  • D: Difficulty speaking
  • W: weakness/numbness
  • T: Temporary paralysis
  • B: Blurred Vision
  • N: Nausea
  • S: Slurred speech

  • Don't Wait — Treat Bad Neurological Signs
    		•  Identify and treat embolic source (anticoagulation for AF if appropriate)
  • acute reperfusion if eligible.
    		•
    Systemic hypoperfusion Global cerebral hypoperfusion from systemic circulatory failure (not a focal vessel occlusion). Arrhythmias
  • reduced cardiac output
  • severe bleeding
  • shock.
    		•  Diffuse cerebral dysfunction
  • can cause watershed infarcts
  • variable focal signs depending on severity.
    		•  Treat underlying systemic cause (cardiac support
  • volume/blood replacement).
    		•
    Hemorrhagic stroke Bleeding into brain or subarachnoid space → mass effect
  • edema
  • and tissue injury → <20% of strokes.
    •      		Chronic hypertension
  • bleeding disorders
  • drug use (e.g., amphetamines
  • cocaine)
  • vascular malformations
  • aneurysm rupture.
    		•  Sudden severe headache (especially SAH)
  • vomiting
  • decreased consciousness
  • focal deficits
  • rapid neurologic deterioration.
    		•  Management is supportive and surgical when indicated (control blood pressure
  • neurosurgical evacuation
  • aneurysm repair/endovascular treatment).
    		•
    Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) Bleeding into the subarachnoid space
  • often from ruptured aneurysm → presents with "thunderclap" headache.
    		• Ruptured cerebral aneurysm
  • vascular malformations.
    		•  Sudden severe headache
  • blurred vision
  • loss of consciousness
  • nuchal rigidity may occur.
    		•  Emergency neurosurgical / endovascular repair
  • manage intracranial pressure and complications.
    		•
    Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) Bleeding into brain parenchyma forming a hematoma with high morbidity/mortality. Chronic/uncontrolled hypertension
  • coagulopathy
  • illicit stimulant use
  • vascular malformations.
    		•  Rapid onset headache
  • vomiting
  • decreased consciousness
  • hemiplegia
  • severity depends on hematoma size/location.
    		•  Urgent supportive care
  • blood pressure control
  • possible surgical evacuation depending on location/size.
    		•
    Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA) Transient neurologic dysfunction from brief ischemia without infarction
  • high short-term stroke risk.
    •      		Same vascular risk factors as stroke (age
  • diabetes
  • HTN
  • smoking
  • prior stroke/TIA).
    		•  Brief weakness/numbness
  • speech disturbance
  • visual symptoms that resolve quickly.
    		•  Urgent evaluation and secondary prevention (antiplatelet/anticoagulant
  • risk-factor modification) TIA is a warning sign.
